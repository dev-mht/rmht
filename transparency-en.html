01Why this page exists

A scanner finding is neither proof of fraud nor a formality to wave away.

Automated analysers read source code and flag anything that could be dangerous. They are right to do so, and their findings on $RMHT are, for the most part, technically accurate.

What they cannot do is cross-check the code against the actual state of the chain, or recognise a mechanism that does not match the patterns they know. This page closes that gap: every finding, what it says exactly, what is true in it, and what you can verify yourself in one command.

The rule we hold ourselves to

No finding is dismissed as a "false positive" without an explanation. Where a risk is real, it is written here as such.

03The feedGovernor role

The only power that survives renouncement. It is real, and we own that.

Flagged: "Privileged feedGovernor can replace the price oracle with an arbitrary contract" — rated critical

This finding is valid. We do not dispute it.

What the role can do — the list is complete

Three functions carry onlyFeedGovernor, and only three. Search the term in the verified source and count them yourself:

FunctionEffect
updatePriceFeeds(address, address)Replaces the two Chainlink feed addresses (ETH/USD and sequencer)
transferFeedGovernor(address)Proposes a successor. Two-step: the recipient must call acceptFeedGovernor()
renounceFeedGovernor()Extinguishes the role permanently

What the role cannot do

  • Transfer, mint or burn a single RMHT
  • Touch the Uniswap pool, frozen by lockConfig()
  • Withdraw the liquidity position
  • Change airdrop allocations, unlock dates, or the LP fee destination
  • Call rescueTokens() or excludeFromRewards() — those are onlyOwner, therefore dead

No path lets the feedGovernor award itself tokens.

The real risk, stated without shrinking it

updatePriceFeeds() checks that the candidate address is a contract, exposes decimals(), and returns a strictly positive ETH/USD price. Those checks rule out an invalid address, but not a malicious contract that answers the interface correctly while returning a false price.

Concretely: a hostile holder of this role could distort getMarketCap() and therefore bring forward or delay the vault's milestone releases — 500,000,000 RMHT, 50% of supply. They could not take those tokens; they go to holders through the reward mechanism. But they could manipulate the schedule. That is a real power.

Why there is no timelock

A mandatory delay before a feed change would have reintroduced the very risk the role exists to cover: if an oracle breaks today, the protocol must be repairable today, not in forty-eight hours. The safeguards sit elsewhere: sanity checks on the candidate address, an event on every change, two-step transfer, and permanent extinction available.

Where it is held, and how it ends

The role is held by 0xC90e404A9f2ea55cbD2C81cf0054FAe4A721889a, a hardware wallet. renounceFeedGovernor() will remove it forever, bringing the contract to a state with no residual power at all. That call will be made once the feeds are considered durably stable, and it will be announced publicly.

04The $MHT V2 precedent

The reason feedGovernor exists is not theoretical. It happened, to this project.

On $MHT V2, our token on BNB Chain, the milestone trigger relied on Chainlink Automation. Two problems compounded: a configuration mistake on our side — the address registered was the Chainlink Registry rather than the upkeep's Forwarder, while the actual caller of performUpkeep() is always the Forwarder — and, discovered in the same period, Chainlink discontinuing that Automation service.

The contract had already renounced ownership, and the corrective function was onlyOwner. There was no way left to repair it. The result: roughly 76% of $MHT V2's supply is frozen permanently. Not stolen — frozen, unreachable, forever.

What we say about it

The initial configuration mistake is ours, and on its own it was enough to break the mechanism: performUpkeep() could never have succeeded, even with a Chainlink service in perfect working order.

Chainlink's migration to CRE, and the discontinuation of the Automation service that came with it, would have stopped milestone triggering anyway — a second cause, independent of the first. But chronologically, it is that migration that made us reopen the file and discover our own mistake, which had gone unnoticed until then.

So we are not putting the blame on a third party: Chainlink evolved its product line, which is its right, and our contract would have been broken even without that.

So the risk feedGovernor covers is not "a malicious governor". It is: an oracle changes, disappears or is deprecated, and nobody can react. Robinhood Chain is a recent chain, and Chainlink has officially stopped extending its Sequencer Uptime Feeds to new networks. The odds that a feed address must change one day are not negligible.

Without this role, the day the ETH/USD feed breaks, the price calculation reverts fail-closed, no milestone can ever trigger again, and the vault's 500,000,000 RMHT are lost to everyone. We chose the risk that can be watched over the one that is irreversible.

And $MHT today

A version 3 of the $MHT contract is already in development, written on the same guiding principle as $RMHT: no dependency on Chainlink Automation or any third party for triggering, price computed from an internal TWAP read directly from the pool, and milestones self-triggered by any caller. The timing and terms of the $MHT V2 to V3 transition will be the subject of a dedicated announcement.

05"LP locked: 0%"

The displayed figure is wrong, and the reality is stronger than what it measures.

Flagged: Percentage of LP locked: 0%

Aggregators look for a known locker contract (PinkLock, Unicrypt, Team Finance...) holding LP tokens. They find none, and conclude the liquidity is not locked. This is the most widely read rug signal in the space, so let us be precise.

The $RMHT liquidity position is a Uniswap v3 NFT, tokenId 1004464, held by the RMHTLiquidityCustodian contract (0x50336e2a1396364895702a5222faC47bD0d38407).

That contract has no function capable of moving the NFT out or reducing the liquidity. This is not a promise: decreaseLiquidity(), burn(), any outgoing NFT transfer, any selfdestruct and any proxy mechanism are deliberately absent from the code, and the code is publicly verified. The only function that moves anything is collect(), which gathers swap fees and sends them to an address fixed in contract state — callable by anyone, but with a destination that can no longer be changed now that ownership is renounced.

Difference from a standard lock

A time lock expires on a date, after which liquidity can be withdrawn. Here there is no date: no code path exists, at any point in time, to withdraw this liquidity. It is permanent rather than dated.

06The market-cap gap

The aggregators' number and the one that triggers milestones do not measure the same thing.

Aggregators display a market cap computed on the full one-billion supply of RMHT. The contract computes getMarketCap() on the eligible supply: total supply minus burned tokens, minus tokens held by the contract itself (the vault), minus those sitting in the pool.

The gap is large and structural: at launch, aggregators showed roughly $513,000 where the contract computed roughly $34,300.

Do not confuse the two

Milestones trigger every $500,000 of eligible supply, on the value computed by the contract — never on a figure displayed by a third-party site. Seeing an aggregator show $513,000 does not mean a milestone has been reached.

The counter on this site's home page reads getMarketCap() directly from the chain. It is the only value the protocol acts on, and you can read it yourself.

No terminal needed

In the RMHT contract's "Read contract" tab, find getMarketCap, click it, then Read. The number returned is in whole dollars. Right next to it, nextMilestoneUSD gives you the next milestone to reach.

07Manipulating the price to trigger a milestone

A real risk on a young pool, and the three obstacles set against it.

Flagged: "Milestone market-cap price manipulation remains possible with low-liquidity pools"

Correct. On a shallow pool, buying enough to move the price costs less than on a mature one. An actor seeking to trigger a milestone artificially could try to push the price up.

Four mechanisms make the operation expensive and unrewarding:

  • The 30-minute TWAP. The price used is not the spot price but a time-weighted average over 1,800 seconds. A momentary spike barely moves that average: the distorted price must be held for a full half hour.
  • Three spaced confirmations. A milestone requires 3 confirmations, at least 10 minutes apart, within a 2-hour window. The manipulation must therefore survive three separate readings across time.
  • Twenty-four hours between milestones. MILESTONE_COOLDOWN enforces a full day before a next milestone can be armed. Chaining several milestones off one manipulation is therefore impossible: each requires repeating the whole operation, a day later.
  • The cost is real and the exit is expensive. Pushing the price up means actually buying; selling back into a shallow pool then drives the price down against you. And what a milestone releases does not go to the manipulator: tokens go to holders, pro rata, through the reward mechanism.

We do not claim manipulation is impossible. It is made slow, visible and economically losing — and its benefit does not accrue to whoever attempts it.

08The remaining findings

Informational items, covered here so that none is left unanswered.

FindingAnswer
External TWAP and oracle dependency can cause milestone denial of service Correct, and deliberate. If the oracle does not answer or returns an aberrant value, the calculation reverts rather than returning a false number (fail-closed): no milestone triggers on doubtful data. That behaviour is exactly what makes the feedGovernor role necessary.

Why is the "transfer + 9 transactions" line so expensive?

Most of that line is not an incident: it is the TWAP oracle pre-sizing, which requires reserving 1,801 storage slots in the pool. That single operation accounts for roughly 36 million units of gas — far more than the four deployments combined.

The failed transaction you will see in the history

One transaction in our deployment failed, and we would rather explain it than let someone find it. The deployment tool freezes the parameters of all its transactions at simulation time. But the liquidity NFT id is a global Uniswap counter, shared with the whole chain: between our simulation and the mint landing in a block, 17 other positions were created by third parties. The script therefore asked to transfer an NFT we did not own, and the transaction was rejected.

The remaining steps were replayed one by one with the correct id. Cost of the incident: 0.0000116 ETH of burnt gas, about three cents. No funds lost, no address changed, no protocol parameter affected — the five addresses announced before launch are exactly the ones deployed.

Allocation unlocking: two different regimes

The airdrop unlocks at whichever comes first: either milestonesReached() hits 5 milestones, or the unlockTime date arrives — 4 September 2027 at 00:53 UTC, one year to the day after deployment. A project that performs therefore releases the airdrop sooner; a flat market cannot block it indefinitely.

The founder allocation has no early unlock. unlockedAmount() returns 0 before the date and 50,000,000 after, with no exception. No milestone accelerates it. Holders can therefore be served before the founder, never the other way round.

In both cases, milestone rewards are independent of that lock: they accrue and stay claimable at any time, through claimReward().
